## The short answer

<InShort>In one well-known study, it took people about **66 days** on average for a new daily behavior to feel automatic. For some it took 18 days, for others an estimated 254.</InShort>

So if a habit still feels like effort after three weeks, nothing is wrong with you. You are simply in the middle of the process.

## Where “21 days” came from

The 21-day idea is usually traced back to a self-help book from 1960 by a plastic surgeon, Maxwell Maltz.<Cite n={1} /> He noticed that his patients seemed to need at least about three weeks to get used to a change, such as a new face after surgery.<Cite n={3} />

Over time, “at least about 21 days” turned into “it takes 21 days.” That is a catchy line, but it was never a research finding about habits.

## What the study did

In a study published in 2009, researchers at University College London asked 96 volunteers to pick one healthy eating, drinking or activity behavior and do it every day in the same situation, for example after breakfast, for 12 weeks.<Cite n={2} />

Each day, people rated how automatic the behavior felt: did they do it without thinking? The researchers then looked at how long it took for that feeling to level off.

## What it found

<Stats items={[['66', 'days, on average'], ['18', 'days, fastest'], ['254', 'days, slowest (estimated)']]} />

- **It took longer than most people expect.** The typical time was a little over two months.
- **People were very different.** The time to level off ranged from 18 days to an estimated 254 days.
- **Missing one day did not ruin it.** Missing a single chance to do the behavior did not really change how the habit formed. Doing it consistently mattered more.

## Why some habits take longer

In the study, simple behaviors, like drinking a glass of water, reached their peak faster than harder ones, like doing 50 sit-ups.<Cite n={3} /> That makes sense: the more effort a behavior needs, the longer it takes before it runs on its own.

The situation matters too. Repeating a behavior in the same situation each day is how it became more automatic in the study.<Cite n={2} /> A fixed time and place gives your brain a clear cue to link it to.

## What this means for you

- **Plan for months, not weeks.** Give a new habit at least two months before you judge it.
- **Start small.** A smaller version is easier to repeat on busy days, and repetition is what builds the habit.
- **Pick a fixed cue.** “Right after my morning coffee” works better than “sometime today.”
- **Do not panic after a miss.** One missed day is normal. Pick it back up the next day.
- **Make progress visible.** Seeing your days fill up can help you notice that you are getting there.
